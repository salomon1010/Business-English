# Presentation brief — Petrocertif meeting

**INTERNAL.** Rehearsal notes and objection prep. The deck already carries a
speaker note on every slide; this is what the notes cannot hold.

---

## Before you walk in

**Send nothing in advance except the proposal.** If he reads the playbook first
he will arrive with operational questions and you will never get to the
argument.

**Open the app on your own phone before the meeting** and leave it on the
workplace scenario screen. Slides 5 to 9 are far stronger if you hand him the
phone instead of pointing at a picture. If there is no signal, the deck still
works — that is why the screenshots are in it.

**Know your three numbers cold:** 12 workplace scenarios, 12 interview coaches,
15 languages. If you fumble a number about your own product, everything else
gets doubted.

**Decide beforehand what you will accept.** The smallest yes is *one cohort,
four weeks, free*. Do not leave with "we'll think about it" — leave with either
a date for week 0 or a specific reason it is a no.

---

## The two-part opening

Say both parts in the first minute. The honesty is not a weakness here, it is
the reason he will give you real feedback instead of politeness.

> "Two things today. First, I want to propose a pilot — one cohort, four weeks,
> free. Second, and honestly: this is the first time I present this properly,
> and I want you to tell me where it is weak, because I am taking the same
> presentation to academies in Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya and Canada."

Then move on. Do not dwell on it.

---

## The one slide that must land

**Slide 8 — the report that names the gap.** Everything before it is context,
everything after it is logistics. Slow down, and say the sentence plainly:

> "A score tells you that you failed. This tells you what was missing and gives
> you the sentence that would have carried it."

If you are running out of time, cut slides 6, 7 and 9 — not slide 8.

---

## Lead with what their students told you

Before any objection comes up, say this yourself, early — on slide 13 at the
latest:

> "The first feedback from your students was that the app opened above their
> level. So it now starts with a one-minute check, and anyone not ready begins
> with Foundations: three sentences a day, French under every line, fifteen
> days. It was built in a week, from what they said."

That sentence does three jobs. It shows you listen. It turns a weakness they
already know about into evidence. And it pre-empts the hardest question in the
room — *"our learners are not at that level"* — because you have already
answered it.

## Objections, and honest answers

**"Our learners' English is too weak for this."**
It was. Their students said so, and Foundations exists because of it. Every
learner now starts with the check; nobody is put in front of a screen they
cannot follow. And the measurement changed with it: for A1–A2 learners the
before-and-after is the same three sentences recorded four weeks apart, not a
scenario they could not attempt.


**"Will they actually use it?"**
Some will not. That is exactly what activation and weekly-return measure, and it
is the main reason the pilot is worth running at all. Do not promise engagement
you cannot evidence — you have no data yet, and he knows it.

**"What does it cost after the pilot?"**
"I do not know yet, and I would rather find out from what the pilot shows than
guess in front of you." Then ask *him*: "What would make this obviously worth
paying for?" That is your pricing research and it is free.

**"Our learners have poor internet / expensive data."**
Honest split: the app works offline after first load. The scoring and Executive
Polish need internet, and it is a short clip, not video. Do not overclaim — he
will test it.

**"Is this an English certification?"**
No. Say it flatly. It is a completion certificate for the app's own 84-session
programme, it is not IELTS or a CEFR level, and written English is not assessed.
This is also the moment to raise his course-page wording (see below).

**"Can you do our other six disciplines?"**
Not today. Welder, pipefitter and boilermaker exist; electrical, civil,
instrumentation, renewable and metal structures do not. "The pilot decides which
one gets built next, and you choose it."

**"Who else uses it?"**
Nobody yet, at institution level. "You would be the first, which is exactly why
you get to shape it and why it costs you nothing."

---

## The wording conversation

His course page promises **"certification in written and spoken English."** The
app does not do that. Raise it — privately if you prefer, but raise it before
the cohort starts, because a learner who enrols expecting a written-English
certificate complains to *his* academy.

Suggested replacement, already in the proposal:

> *Strengthen your spoken professional English through guided daily practice and
> workplace speaking simulations, with a record of the speaking work you have
> completed — via the BE Mastery app by Lomonec.*

Frame it as protecting him, because it is.

---

## Close with two questions, then be quiet

1. **"If you did not know me, what would stop you saying yes?"**
2. **"Which slide would you forward to your partners — and which would you
   delete?"**

Do not fill the silence after either one. Write both answers down the same day,
before you rationalise them. These two answers are worth more to you than the
pilot itself, because they are what you take to the next fifteen institutions.

---

## What to send afterwards, and when

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Same day | One short thank-you, the proposal PDF again, and the two things he asked you to change |
| On yes | The playbook, and a request for the four names |
| Week 0 | The learner guide to every participant, with the induction invite |
| Weekly | One short email with the figure. Never a chase, never through his staff |

Do not send the playbook before he says yes. It answers questions he has not
asked yet, and it makes a free four-week trial look like an implementation
project.
